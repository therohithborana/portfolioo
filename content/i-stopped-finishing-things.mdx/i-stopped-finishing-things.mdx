I bought a book a few months ago. A really popular self-help one.

I read half of it. And then I just… stopped.

I just didn’t feel like picking it up anymore. It sat on my table for weeks, looking at me.

And the guilt showed up right on time.

*I paid for this.*\
*Maybe the real value is in the second half?*\
*I’ve already finished half, it’d be a waste to stop now.*

So I kept it on the table. Not reading it. Not giving it away. Just feeling bad about it every few days.

Then it hit me that I wasn’t guilty about the book. I was guilty about the money and the hours I’d already put in. Which, funnily enough, I can’t get back whether I finish it or not :)

---

## So that’s a thing, apparently

It’s called the **sunk cost fallacy**, and here’s the simple version:

You’ve already spent something (money, time, effort) and you can’t get it back. And then you let that past spending decide what you do next.

That’s it. That’s the whole fallacy.

But what if we just ask a simple question: *if we were starting today with zero history, would we still choose this?*


---

## A heavier example

Okay, let’s take another example.

Think about how many people in India finish a degree they don’t like. Not because they want to. Because they’ve already done two years of it. Because the fees are paid. Because relatives are asking.

“Just finish it, then see.”

I get it. I really do. When you come from a middle-class Indian household, wasting money just feels wrong. And walking away feels like saying all that sacrifice was for nothing.

But I think the sacrifice has already happened. The only question is whether to add more years on top of it.

Then there’s UPSC.

Some people spend five, six years preparing. Attempt after attempt. No plan B. And at some point, they’ve spent so much time that a plan B doesn’t even occur to them. They don’t even realise the savings are running out. And so is the time.

I feel the hardest part is that every year makes quitting feel more expensive. Leaving doesn’t feel like a choice anymore. It feels like you’ll lose everything.

And that’s how the trap works. We let our past decide the future. And the future keeps getting smaller.

---

## But wait, not everything should be quit

Okay, so does that mean we should just quit everything? No way.

Persistence is real. Some of the best things we ever do are the ones that feel terrible in the middle, but in the end it’s all worth it.

But how do you tell a **dip** from a **dead end**?

I don’t have a perfect answer (I’m 22, haha). But here’s what I’ve been thinking:

A dip is when you’re struggling, but you’re still learning something. With the pain, there’s direction. There’s progress. Say you’re learning guitar. You couldn’t play a few chords last month, and now you can. That’s a dip.

A dead end is when nothing is changing even though you’re trying. Same problem, same frustration, no new information. Like there’s nothing real left to solve.

And one more thing, because it matters. **Switching costs are real.** Leaving something and starting something new costs you time, energy and momentum. But those are *future* costs, so they belong in the equation. That’s very different from sunk cost, which is the past.

So “I’ve already spent 6 months on this” is sunk cost. Not a reason.\
“Restarting will cost me 3 months of learning a new tool” is a switching cost. Totally a reason.

Hard to tell them apart in the moment, but the trick is just asking which one it is. Is this about the past, or about what’s ahead?

---

## What I’m trying to do about it

I’ve started a small toolkit. Nothing fancy.

**1. The zero-base test.** Same one from before. “Starting from scratch today, would I choose this?” If the honest answer is no, that’s a signal.

**2. Kill criteria, decided before I start.** This is my favourite. Before beginning something, I write down what would make me stop. “If I’m not enjoying this by chapter 5, I’m done.” “If this project doesn’t have users after 3 months, I move on.” Because *future me*, deep in the project, is a terrible judge. He’s emotionally invested and will argue for staying.

**3. Time-boxing.** Give it a fixed window. Two hours on this bug. Two weeks on this idea. When the time’s up, I check in with myself honestly, then decide whether to extend. Extending is allowed! But it has to be a decision, not a drift.

**4. Ask a friend with no stake.** Someone who didn’t spend the money or the time. They’ll usually say the obvious thing in about five seconds. (And it’s annoying how often they’re right.)


---

## So, did I finish the book?

No.

I gave it to a friend who actually wanted to read it. It felt weirdly good. The book is being used now, which is more than it was doing on my table :)

And I’m not feeling guilty about it anymore. The money’s gone either way. The hours are gone either way.

Honestly, that’s the one thing I keep coming back to.

**Time is the one thing that doesn’t come back.**

Money can be earned again. A degree can be restarted. A project can be rebuilt. But the hours I spend on something I’d never choose today, those are gone for good.

So maybe at 22 the goal isn’t to finish everything. Maybe it’s to be a bit more careful about what I give my time to. And to stay for the right reasons.

Stop things on purpose. Continue things on purpose.

Just never by accident.
